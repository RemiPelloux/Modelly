\section{Correctness and reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}

\subsection{Same seed, same digest}

A determinism suite runs seventeen examples (the fourteen bundled examples
and the three split-run reference models of \cref{sec:split}, distinct from the seventeen
zoo models of \cref{sec:zoo}) for ten steps from a fixed seed and
digests their collected data and final agent state (SHA-256 of a canonical serialisation).
The digest must be identical between the compiled and the pure-Python builds, between a
straight run and a run saved and resumed from a checkpoint at the midpoint (fifteen of the
seventeen examples can be checkpointed), and against reference digests stored in the
repository; the three split-run models must also give it split into 2, 4 and 16 partitions.
Ten examples are compared exactly. Seven pass through functions whose last bit legitimately differs between
platforms' maths libraries (\code{exp}, \code{log}, non-integer powers, \code{arctan2}, BLAS
reductions) and are digested at ten significant digits; one of these depends on geographic
libraries and is compared only on the platform that recorded it. Property-based tests check
invariants of columns, cohorts, cell spaces, checkpoints and experiment designs on random
inputs.

\paragraph{Three guarantees, by scope.} Determinism, numerical reproducibility and bitwise
reproducibility are different properties, and the suite tests each within a stated scope.
\emph{Determinism on one build}: the same code, inputs and seed give the same bytes, run
straight or resumed, compiled or pure, in one process or split across workers; this is what
\cmd{modelly verify} and the split-run contract of \cref{sec:split} promise, and it is
exact. \emph{Numerical reproducibility across platforms}: every example gives the same
digest at ten significant digits on every platform and interpreter tested, which is the
guarantee a reader of a published study can rely on when rerunning it on another machine.
\emph{Bitwise reproducibility across platforms}: holds for the examples whose arithmetic
is integer or exactly rounded, and is reported example by example rather than claimed in
general, since the last bits of \code{exp}, \code{log}, powers, \code{arctan2} and BLAS
reductions depend on the platform's libraries.

\paragraph{Where the checks ran.} \Cref{tab:determinism} states where each part of this is
exercised. Every check ran on the development machine (Linux x86-64, CPython 3.15), and
the suite was also run, for this version, on nine hosted builds: Linux, macOS and Windows,
each under CPython 3.12, 3.13 and 3.14, with the compiled modules built on each (the
\code{platform-digests} workflow, run on 29 October 2026 at a commit whose \code{modelly}
package is the tag's; the paper's \code{data/platform\_runs.json} records the runs and
\code{data/platform\_digests/} holds the digests). The determinism suite passed on
all nine (50 or 51 tests each; the two examples that cannot be checkpointed skip their
resume test, and the geographic example's golden digest is compared on Linux only). Beyond
the rounded comparison, each build also wrote the exact digest of every example, and
\cref{tab:platform-digests} compares them: twelve of the seventeen examples, including two
of the seven that the golden suite compares at ten digits, give bit-identical digests on
all nine builds; the other five differ across operating systems, and two of them (boid
flockers and Sugarscape G1MT) across interpreter versions on the same operating system as
well, while agreeing at ten significant digits everywhere the golden suite compares them.
The same workflow then ran twice more on fifteen builds, five runner architectures (Linux
x86-64 and ARM64, macOS ARM64 and x86-64, Windows x86-64) by the three interpreters: once
from the checkout, compiled on each runner, and once from the released 1.4.2 wheels
installed from PyPI with the checkout's package directory removed, so that the tests import
the wheel (\code{data/platform\_digests\_15/} and \code{data/platform\_digests\_wheels/}).
The determinism suite passed on all thirty, every wheel imported with its five compiled
modules present, and the released wheels give bit-identical digests on all fifteen builds
for thirteen of the seventeen examples (\cref{tab:platform-digests}); the four that differ
are the same ones as before, less Sugarscape G1MT, and they differ mostly along operating
system and architecture lines (macOS against Linux and Windows; macOS ARM64 against macOS
x86-64), with one interpreter-level exception (boid flockers on Windows under 3.13), while
agreeing at ten significant digits wherever the suite compares them. This is the check the first version
of this paper lacked: the published wheels, installed as a user would install them, on the
five architectures they are built for.
The MPI workflow (\code{mpi.yml}) ran the distributed suite and the partition-independence
tests under Open MPI on Linux and macOS with 2 and 4 ranks and under MS-MPI on Windows with
2 ranks, all passing (87, 87 and 85 tests); and the bundle workflow (\code{platforms.yml})
bundled three studies on Linux and verified them on macOS and Windows with
\cmd{modelly verify}, outputs matching on both.

\subsection{Validation of the scientific methods}
\label{sec:validation}

\paragraph{Sobol indices on the Ishigami function.} The Ishigami function
\citep{ishigami1990importance},
\begin{equation}
  f(x) = \sin x_1 + a \sin^2 x_2 + b\, x_3^4 \sin x_1, \qquad x_i \sim
  \mathcal{U}(-\pi, \pi), \quad a = 7,\ b = 0.1,
\end{equation}
has known indices. With $V_1 = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + b\pi^4/5\bigr)^2$, $V_2 = a^2/8$ and
$V_{13} = b^2\pi^8\bigl(\tfrac{1}{18} - \tfrac{1}{50}\bigr)$, the variance is $V = V_1 + V_2 +
V_{13} = 13.845$, and
\begin{equation}
  S = \Bigl(\tfrac{V_1}{V}, \tfrac{V_2}{V}, 0\Bigr) = (0.314, 0.442, 0), \qquad
  S_T = \Bigl(\tfrac{V_1 + V_{13}}{V}, \tfrac{V_2}{V}, \tfrac{V_{13}}{V}\Bigr)
  = (0.558, 0.442, 0.244).
\end{equation}
With $N = 4{,}096$ base samples ($20{,}480$ evaluations) and the Jansen estimators of
\cref{eq:jansen}, \modelly{} estimates from seed 1 the first-order indices
$\hat S = (0.313, 0.442, -0.003)$ and the total indices $\hat S_T = (0.557, 0.442, 0.244)$ (\code{paper/data/ishigami.json}, written by
\code{paper/scripts/ishigami.py}), and every analytical value lies inside the bootstrap
interval of its estimate. The bootstrap resamples the rows of a scrambled Sobol design as
if they were independent, which they are not, so we measured the uncertainty the proper
way as well: by repeating the estimate on 32 independent scramblings of the sequence
(seeds 1 to 32), at $N = 1{,}024$ and $4{,}096$ (\code{paper/scripts/sobol\_scramblings.py},
\cref{tab:sobol-scramblings}). At $N = 4{,}096$ the standard deviation of an index across
scramblings is 0.0017 to 0.0046, the 2.5--97.5\% range of the 32 estimates contains the
analytical value for all six indices, and the row bootstrap's half-width is 3.1 to 5.9
times $1.96$ times that standard deviation (3.5 to 6.8 times at $N = 1{,}024$): the row
bootstrap is conservative here, overstating the randomised-quasi-Monte-Carlo uncertainty
several-fold rather than understating it, and all 32 of its intervals contain the
analytical value for every index. The scramblings' spread, not the row bootstrap, is the
uncertainty we recommend reporting; \modelly{} keeps the row bootstrap as the cheap
default (one design evaluated once) and a study that needs a calibrated interval should
repeat the analysis over seeds, as this table does.

\paragraph{Parameter recovery and coverage of ABC-SMC.} Synthetic data are generated from a
stochastic SIR model with $\beta = 0.4$ and $\gamma = 0.1$ and fitted with uniform priors. In
the documentation's worked example (100 particles, six generations, 2,200 model runs, final
tolerance 83.6), the posterior means are $\hat\beta = 0.423$ and $\hat\gamma = 0.098$, with 95\%
highest-density intervals $[0.33, 0.55]$ and $[0.067, 0.139]$. One dataset and one run are an
anecdote, so we measured coverage at three true points per model, spread across the priors:
for each of 200 replicates per point we simulated a fresh dataset from the true parameters (its
own data seed) and calibrated it with the test suite's settings
(30 particles for the SIR model, 40 for a stochastic predator-prey model with a log-uniform
prior on the predation rate, six generations). These are the small settings that keep the
tests fast, not recommended ones: with so few particles the highest-density intervals are
coarse, so the coverage measures the method under the tests' budget. \Cref{tab:recovery} gives how often the true
value lies in the posterior's 95\% highest-density interval.

Nominal coverage is guaranteed only on average over parameters drawn from the prior; at a
fixed true point a well-calibrated posterior can cover more or less often. Coverage is 94 to
100\% for a nominal 95\% at all six true points (the lowest Clopper--Pearson
bound is 0.90), so the intervals are not too narrow and, for $\gamma$ at 0.1 and 0.2 and five of the six
predator-prey parameters, conservative (lower bound at least 0.96), as expected of ABC: a non-zero tolerance widens the posterior. With the true parameters drawn
from the prior instead, 500 datasets per model (\code{paper/scripts/prior\_coverage.py},
\code{paper/data/prior\_coverage.json}), coverage is 94.2\% [91.8, 96.1] for $\gamma$, 98.4\%
and 96.8\% for the two predator-prey rates, and 91.2\% [88.4, 93.5] for $\beta$, which is below nominal at
these small test settings. At the 100 particles of the documented example
(\cref{lst:abc}; \code{prior\_coverage.py --particles 100}), coverage is nominal or conservative
for all four parameters: 95.8\% [93.7, 97.4] for $\beta$, 96.4\% [94.4, 97.9] for $\gamma$, 99.0\%
and 97.2\% for the predator-prey rates.

That 100 particles suffice for these two models says nothing about a third: the particles a
calibration needs depend on the number of parameters, the model's stochasticity, the
summaries, the tolerance schedule and how well the data identify the parameters. We
therefore recommend a protocol rather than a number, and run it here
(\code{paper/scripts/abc\_sweep.py}; \cref{tab:abc-sweep} in the appendix): calibrate the
same 500 prior-drawn datasets per model at increasing particle counts, 30, 60, 100 and
200, and watch the diagnostics converge: the width of the 95\% interval, the posterior's
effective sample size, the last generation's acceptance rate, and, on synthetic data, the
coverage. On the SIR model $\beta$'s coverage rises from 91.2\% at 30 particles to 93.6\%
at 60, 95.8\% at 100 and 95.2\% at 200, and $\gamma$'s from 94.2 to 96.4, 96.4 and 98.0\%;
the interval widens from 0.265 to 0.285, 0.294 and 0.297 for $\beta$ as more particles
resolve its tails, which is where the under-coverage at 30 came from: a 30-particle
highest-density interval is too short, not misplaced. On the predator-prey model coverage
is 94.8 to 99.8\% at every count, nominal at 30 and conservative from 60 on, as expected of
ABC with a non-zero tolerance. The effective sample size stays at 0.89 to 0.95 of the particles
and the last acceptance rate at 17 to 19\% at every count, so the kernel adapts rather than
starves, and the cost grows linearly (625 to 4,032 runs for the SIR model). The interval's
width stops changing between 100 and 200 particles (by 1\%) for every parameter, which is
the stopping rule we propose: run the ladder on the model at hand with synthetic data from
the prior, and stop at the count beyond which the interval's width and the coverage no
longer move; for these two models that is 100, the documented example's setting, and the
test suite's 30 and 40 are below it. Accuracy depends
on how well 30 steps identify the parameters. At four of the six points the mean signed error of the posterior mean is within 10\% of the
truth (its mean absolute error is 6 to 14\%); for the predation rate at 0.01 they overestimate it by
16\% (its posterior is right-skewed under the log-uniform prior; the median errs by 15\%), and
for the slow epidemic ($\beta = 0.25$, $\gamma = 0.05$, $R_0 = 5$, whose infections peak at the last of the 30 observed steps in 85\% of the datasets, as
\code{recovery.py analyse} reports) by 24\% and 81\%: the data say little about these values, the intervals
are wide, and they still cover the truth 94 and 96\% of the time.
The test suite asserts recovery on three fixed seeds per model, with a 25\% criterion on the
posterior mean and the truth inside the interval; for the skewed predation rate it uses the
median.

\paragraph{Importance weights.} A dedicated test checks \cref{eq:abc-weights} directly: from a
population crowding the lower bound of a uniform prior, 40,000 proposals reweighted by their
importance weights must be uniform within 8\% in every bin next to the bound. It guards
against a subtle error: moving a proposal that leaves the support again from the same
ancestor makes the effective proposal density differ from the one in the weights, and
over-weights the bin next to the bound by 17\% in this test; \modelly{} redraws the ancestor
as well \citep{toni2009approximate}.

\paragraph{Executor independence and resumption.} Posteriors and Sobol indices are identical
on the serial, thread and process executors, and an ABC-SMC run gives the same posterior with
the compiled or the pure-Python core. A calibration stopped after a generation, or by its run
budget, and resumed from its checkpoint reaches the same final posterior as an uninterrupted
one.

\paragraph{Reference models.} Each of the seventeen zoo models (\cref{sec:zoo}) has a test
against an analytical or well-established result; the five coupled or real-data ones are
described with their criteria in \cref{app:couple,app:population}. For the age-structured SEIR and Bass
diffusion models the tolerance is derived from the statistic's sampling variance; others use
fixed tolerances (for example 3\% for the energy-budget population around its carrying
capacity) or a heuristic expectation (the number of opinion clusters in the Deffuant model,
about $1/(2\varepsilon)$). Each test checks one point or one regime of its model's
parameter space, the one where an analytical result exists; it is evidence that the model
is implemented as described there, not a validation over the parameter ranges a study
might sweep, and the zoo's role in this paper is as tested starting points, not as
evidence for the framework's claims. A forest fire translated from NetLogo by \cmd{modelly netlogo} burns 8, 15, 56, 89 and
97\% of its trees at tree densities of 50, 55, 59, 63 and 70\% (a $101 \times 101$ grid, mean
of five seeds), the transition at the square lattice's site-percolation threshold
\citep{newman2000efficient} (\code{docs/netlogo.md}).
