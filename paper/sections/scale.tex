\section{Scaling out and connecting to data}
\label{sec:scale}

A study often outgrows one process, or needs its model tied to data: a population too large
for one core, millions of agents whose phases are array operations, parameters to fit by
gradient, a model kept in step with arriving observations, agents on a diffusing resource,
a differential equation inside every agent, a
model too expensive to run thousands of times, a realistic starting population, or a model
that colleagues should try without installing anything. \modelly{} covers each of these with
the same model code. For each capability this section gives the mechanism, what was measured
and the conditions under which it holds; \cref{tab:scale} collects the numbers, and \cref{app:scale} has the designs, the measurement conditions
and the file behind every one of them. The measurements were made on the laptop of
\cref{sec:performance} while it was shared with other work and without the fixed frequency of
\cref{sec:playbooks} (except the timings of equations inside agents), and \cref{sec:threats} says which are repeated runs and which are single
runs; the conclusions that matter (byte-identical outputs, order-of-magnitude speed-ups) do not
turn on the last digit of a timing. Each capability is presented as validated functionality
with the performance evidence stated and bounded, not as a general acceleration claim: the
GPU's 28-fold step speed-up holds at ten million agents and shrinks to 1.0 to 1.7 once the
agents are built, and the split run's byte-identical results are a correctness property
whose multi-node speed-up has not been shown.

\subsection{Per-agent random streams and split runs}
\label{sec:split}

A model can give every agent random generators of its own, seeded from the scenario's seed
and the agent's key, so that what an agent draws no longer depends on the activation order or
on the population (the flag is off by default). One run can then be split across local, MPI,
Dask or Ray workers (a grid into blocks with halos, a network by breadth-first banding, a
continuous space by recursive bisection), with ghost agents exchanged before a phase that
reads across a border and movers migrating after a phase that moves them. On three reference
models every reporter and every agent's state is byte-identical to the single-process run at
1, 2, 4 and 16 partitions, under MPI with 2 and 4 ranks and on two Dask workers
(\cref{app:split}). The weak scaling of \cref{tab:scale} comes from single runs on a laptop
shared with other jobs, whose hybrid processor is the main limit: eight copies of the same
work with no communication at all already run at 25\% of one, because beyond the four
performance cores the copies land on efficiency cores, and with 10,000 people per worker the
protocol keeps 40 to 61\% of what the machine gives from four workers on, 80 to 90\% with
40,000 (\cref{tab:scaling}). A split run on one laptop is therefore a check of correctness
more than a speed-up; scaling to the 64 workers of a cluster was not measured, and the Ray
backend is untested.

\subsection{A GPU backend}
\label{sec:gpu}

Column phases, cohort tables and the cell-space operators run on CuPy or JAX with a
\code{numpy} fallback, from counter-based (Philox) random streams that draw the same numbers
on every backend. At ten million agents the grid epidemic's 60 steps take 0.80~s on an
RTX~5050 laptop GPU against 20.5~s for the CPU column variant on one core (medians of five
rounds), 28 times faster (median of the rounds' ratios, 25 to 37), and 123 times faster than the same GPU variant run on \code{numpy}. The GPU variant draws from Philox streams rather than the model's
generator, so it is a different variant of the model from the CPU column one, agreeing with it
in distribution; run on \code{numpy} and on CuPy it gives the same infected series and the same
digest of the final state (\cref{app:gpu}). The comparison is with one CPU core: a sweep of independent replicates can instead run one
per core, which on this 16-core laptop would narrow the GPU's lead in throughput by up to that
factor (not measured). Building ten million agent objects takes 23 to
35~s on either side (83~s for the GPU's first, cold build), so build plus steps gains only 1.0
to 1.7 times; at 20,000 agents the GPU is slower than the CPU (0.15 to 0.16~s against 0.07~s) and the crossover was not
measured; a cohort
table of under 200 rows is about 230 times slower, because kernel launches cost more than its
arithmetic; and Apple Metal was not measured.

\subsection{Differentiable models}
\label{sec:diff}

Column phases traced by JAX give gradients of a model's outputs with respect to its
parameters, exact through normal and uniform draws and approximate through Bernoulli choices
(straight-through, Gumbel-softmax or score-function estimators, each warning that it is one).
Gradient calibration recovers two zoo models' parameters from runs of the agent-based
originals, and an ODE inside every agent is differentiated through the steps of a
fourth-order Runge--Kutta integrator, which recovers the two viral rates of a within-host
model within 0.1\% (\cref{sec:agentode}); and pathwise gradients agree with finite differences (\cref{tab:scale},
\cref{app:diff}); the straight-through estimator is biased and fails that check, by design, so
a gradient through a discrete choice is an approximation whose use the documentation delimits.

\subsection{Live data and data assimilation}
\label{sec:assimilation}

Observations arrive from a replayed table, a growing CSV file, an HTTP endpoint or a message
broker; an ensemble of replicates advances in lock step, a bootstrap particle filter
\citep{gordon1993novel} weights and resamples it, and an ensemble Kalman filter
\citep{evensen2003ensemble} adjusts continuous parameters, both bit-identical for a seed. On
the tutorial's synthetic town the filtered nowcast of the unobserved number infected has a
root-mean-square error of 5.07 people against 69.2 for the unfiltered ensemble, and the
Kalman filter recovers the contact rate as 0.451 for a truth of 0.45 (\cref{app:assimilation});
that is one dataset and one seed, not a coverage study. Message brokers were tested against
fake clients only, and the replicates step in one process.

\subsection{Fields and coupling}
\label{sec:couple}

A field is a property layer with a solver (diffusion, upwind advection and decay, in explicit
sub-steps that refuse an unstable time step, or backward Euler), coupled to differential
equations and stock-flow components under a schedule that is checked when the model is built.
Closed and periodic boundaries conserve mass to a relative $10^{-12}$ and a spreading Gaussian
matches the one-dimensional heat kernel to about $10^{-6}$; two zoo models, foragers on a diffusing nutrient
and an epidemic coupled to hospital capacity, close their budgets to round-off and match their
analytical checks (\cref{app:couple}). Fields need orthogonal grids with uniform spacing, and
upwind advection smears sharp fronts.

\subsection{Differential equations inside agents}
\label{sec:agentode}

When every agent carries an ODE (a viral load inside a host, a physiology, an energy budget),
one \code{solve\_ivp} call per agent costs a Python call per agent and step, and stacking the
agents into one system forces them all onto the step size of the agent that needs the
smallest. \code{solve\_batch} integrates many independent systems at once: it runs SciPy's
Dormand--Prince and Bogacki--Shampine pairs \citep{virtanen2020scipy, dormand1980family,
bogacki1989pair} with SciPy's step selection and error control applied to each system
separately, and evaluates the right-hand side once per stage for all of them. On 200 random
systems of a viral model, with both pairs at two tolerances, every system takes exactly the
accepted steps of its own \code{solve\_ivp} call and ends within a relative $1.9 \times 10^{-11}$ of
it at the looser tolerance and $6 \times 10^{-15}$ at the tighter (\code{paper/data/odes.json}).
\code{AgentODE} keeps the state in an agent class's columns, locates zero crossings per agent
on each step's interpolant (an onset, a clearance), calls back into the model when one occurs,
and hands stiff systems to BDF, Radau or LSODA as one stacked call with a block-diagonal
Jacobian pattern. The same machinery integrates delay equations by the method of steps, keyed
by agent so that newcomers keep their own past (equal to the closed form of
$y'(t) = -a\,y(t-1)$ to a relative $6 \times 10^{-15}$ at model steps of 0.5, 1 and 3), and
stochastic equations by Euler--Maruyama or a derivative-free Milstein scheme with diagonal noise
\citep{kloeden1992numerical}, drawn from the model's generator, whose strong orders on
geometric Brownian motion measure 0.53 and 0.95 against the theoretical $\tfrac12$ and 1. A
mean-field companion runs a model's ODE limit beside it and fits how the gap shrinks with the
population: as $N^{-0.47}$ on the Bass zoo model between 250 and 16,000 households, the
$N^{-1/2}$ of sampling noise.

Timed against the two alternatives on the target-cell-limited model of influenza
\citep{baccam2006kinetics}, three equations per agent over ten days with the Dormand--Prince
pair at a relative tolerance of $10^{-6}$ (best of three runs, one for the loop beyond 1,000 agents, on one core at the fixed
frequency of \cref{sec:playbooks}), \code{solve\_batch} integrates 10,000 agents in 0.25~s where one
\code{solve\_ivp} call per agent takes 54~s, 217 times as long, and 131 times as long on a
population whose rates span orders of magnitude, with the same worst error as the loop at every size
(\code{paper/data/ode\_compare.json}). One stacked \code{solve\_ivp} call is faster when the
agents are alike (0.17~s at 10,000 agents; 2.9~s against 3.4~s at 100,000), since it controls
one step size instead of one per agent; on the diverse population it is level at 10,000 agents
and 1.6 times slower at 100,000 (7.3~s against 4.6~s), since every agent takes the steps of the
one that needs the smallest, which also makes it the more accurate there. In the within-host
zoo model, 100,000 hosts at the epidemic's peak on day 20, \code{AgentODE} advances the hosts
by one day, events included, in 1.07~s against 49.1~s for one \code{solve\_ivp} call per
exposed host, 46 times faster in one run of each, and the two final states agree to
$4 \times 10^{-9}$ of the tolerance (\code{paper/data/ode\_within\_host.json}).

\subsection{Emulators, active learning and history matching}
\label{sec:emulate}

A Gaussian-process or neural emulator trained on an experiment's runs, active learning that
picks the next runs by integrated variance reduction, and history matching in waves
\citep{vernon2010galaxy, andrianakis2015bayesian}. On the age-structured SEIR model four waves
of 20 runs reduce the parameter box to 2.5\%, with the true parameters inside after every wave
(\cref{app:emulate}). Active learning saves the runs it was meant to save on the zoo model and
not on the Branin and Ishigami functions, where a well-spread design is already close to the
best that a variance criterion blind to the outputs can do.

\subsection{Synthetic populations and models on real data}
\label{sec:population}

Iterative proportional fitting, integerisation, households, places within a distance limit and
a contact network with household, school and work layers build a starting population from
aggregate tables; no personal data is bundled, and three small real samples (OpenStreetMap
streets, ESA WorldCover land cover, census tracts) ship with their licences. The synthetic
population reproduces its marginals and starts the SEIR model within its final-size check, and
three zoo models run on the real samples against known answers (\cref{app:population}); the
marginals are made up, so no real joint distribution was tested against.

\subsection{\modelly{} in the browser}
\label{sec:browser}

\cmd{modelly export-web} writes a static site that runs a model with Pyodide in a browser tab,
with Studio's parameter panel, a space view and a reporter plot. The 14 bundled examples
and the 18 zoo models each ran from their export in headless Chromium with the
pure-Python wheel and with the compiled one, 64 of 64 runs (\cref{app:browser}). A cold first
visit is ready after 6.5~s on a 50~Mbit/s line for a model that needs no \code{scipy}, 9.0~s
for one that does, and 19.4 and 33.4~s on a Fast~4G profile, so a first load under ten
seconds is reached on the faster line, with or without \code{scipy}, and not on Fast~4G: Pyodide, \code{numpy}
and \code{pandas} alone are 13.6~MB.
