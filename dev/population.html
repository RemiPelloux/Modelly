Synthetic populations#

modelly.population builds a starting population from aggregate tables: people with their attributes, grouped into households, living at points of their region, going to schools and workplaces, and linked by a contact network. Every step adds columns to one pandas DataFrame with one row per person, so the result creates one agent per row, fills the columns of a column-backed agent set, or summarises into the inputs of a cohort model.

The toolkit needs only numpy and pandas. locate_households takes a GeoSpace, which needs the geo extra (pip install "modelly-abm[geo]"); everything else works on plain coordinates.

People from census marginals#

Census tables give, per region, the number of people at each level of each attribute (age band, sex, household size) but not the joint table. ipf(seed, marginals) fits a joint table to the marginals by iterative proportional fitting; its seed can be a cross-tabulation of a microsample, so the fit keeps the sample’s associations (and its zeros). trs turns the fitted table into whole people: it keeps the floor of every cell and draws the people left over in proportion to the fractional parts (truncate, replicate, sample; Lovelace and Ballas 2013). synthesize_population does both, region by region:

import numpy as np

from modelly import Model
from modelly.population import marginal_errors, synthesize_population

categories = {"age": ["0-17", "18-64", "65+"], "sex": ["f", "m"]}
marginals = {
    "T01": {"age": [210, 600, 190], "sex": [508, 492]},
    "T02": {"age": [90, 330, 80], "sex": [251, 249]},
}
model = Model(rng=42)
people = synthesize_population(categories, marginals, model.rng)
errors = marginal_errors(people, marginals, categories)

Every region gets exactly its total and its first attribute’s counts. The other marginals are met to within the rounding: each count is off by less than the number of levels of the first attribute, and marginal_errors shows by how much. The people are drawn from model.rng, so a seed gives the same population.

modelly.space.geo.ipf and modelly.space.geo.synthesize_population are the same functions; they keep working through 1.x with a deprecation warning and go in 2.0 (see Deprecations).

Whole households by combinatorial optimisation#

IPF fits a table of people; it cannot keep the members of a household together. The combinatorial-optimisation approach (Williamson, Birkin and Rees 1998) does: choose as many records as the region has households from a sample of household records, with replacement, so that their totals match the region’s tables. anneal searches by simulated annealing: it swaps one chosen record for another, keeps every swap that lowers the total absolute error, and keeps a swap that raises it by d with probability exp(-d / T), the temperature T falling at every swap.

import pandas as pd

from modelly.population import Annealing, anneal, total_absolute_error

# One row per sample household: its members per age band and a one-hot of its size.
sample = pd.read_csv("household_sample.csv")
targets = {"children": 210, "adults": 600, "seniors": 190, "size_1": 120, "size_2": 140}
chosen = anneal(sample, targets, Annealing(size=430), model.rng)
total_absolute_error(sample, chosen, targets)
households = sample.iloc[chosen].reset_index(drop=True)

Annealing also sets the number of swaps tried (iterations, default 20,000), the starting temperature (in people) and the cooling factor per swap. The sample is yours: a public-use microdata sample, a survey, or a sample you make up. Modelly ships none.

Households#

assign_households groups the people of each region into households of the sizes in a household-size table. It rounds the table to whole households with trs, grows or shrinks random households one member at a time until they hold exactly the region’s people, gives every household a head among the people who can head one, and spreads the others over the remaining places at random. The people, and so the marginals, do not change.

from modelly.population import HouseholdSpec, assign_households

sizes = [0.28, 0.34, 0.16, 0.14, 0.08]  # shares of households of 1 to 5 people
spec = HouseholdSpec({"T01": sizes, "T02": sizes}, heads=["18-64", "65+"])
people = assign_households(people, spec, model.rng)

A region that needs more heads than it has adults is an error, not a silent fix.

Homes, schools and workplaces#

locate_households puts each household at a random point of its region; all its members share it. assign_places then sends the people who attend a kind of place to the nearest place with room, in random order, never farther than max_distance (in the coordinates’ units, metres for a GeoSpace). People with no place within reach get -1. The places are a DataFrame with x, y and capacity; a person’s place is places.iloc[person.school].

import pandas as pd

from modelly.population import PlaceSpec, assign_places, locate_households
from modelly.space.geo import GeoSpace

space = GeoSpace(tracts, random=model.random)  # tracts indexed like the marginals
people = locate_households(people, space, model.rng)
centres = tracts.to_crs(space.crs).geometry.centroid
schools = pd.DataFrame({"x": centres.x, "y": centres.y, "capacity": 600})
people = assign_places(
    people, schools, PlaceSpec("school", ["0-17"], max_distance=10_000), model.rng
)
people = assign_places(people, workplaces, PlaceSpec("work", ["18-64"]), model.rng)

Contact networks#

contact_network links the people who share a place, one layer per kind of place, in a MultilayerNetwork (see Networks): everybody in a household knows everybody else in it; the pupils of a school are split at random into classes and the workers of a workplace into teams, each a clique. Node i is row i of people. The default layers are household (weight 1), school (0.5, classes of 25) and work (0.25, teams of 10); ContactLayers changes them.

from modelly.population import ContactLayers, contact_network
from modelly.space.networks import contact_matrix

network = contact_network(people, model.rng)
groups = pd.Categorical(people.age, categories=categories["age"]).codes
contacts = contact_matrix(network, groups)  # mean contacts between age groups
pressure = network.adjacency() @ infected  # for a network epidemic

The layered epidemic example runs an epidemic on a network of the same three layers.

Starting the age-structured SEIR model#

The age-structured SEIR zoo model runs on a synthetic population through scenario_from_population, which counts the age groups and measures the contact matrix on the network. Its default scenario does not change.

from modelly.zoo.age_seir.model import AgeSEIR, scenario_from_population

scenario = scenario_from_population(people, network, ("0-17", "18-64", "65+"), rng=5)
seir = AgeSEIR(scenario)
seir.run()
seir.attack_rate(), seir.final_size()

tests/population/test_sample_region.py builds a population of about 20,000 people for the 41 census tracts of Chittenden County, Vermont (the bundled boundaries, see Geospatial models) from illustrative tables, checks that it reproduces every marginal within the rounding tolerance, that households, schools and workplaces respect their sizes, capacities and distances, and that the SEIR model started from it follows its final-size relation.

Privacy#

Everything here works from aggregate tables, and the people it makes are synthetic: no row is a real person, and Modelly bundles no personal data and no microdata. The marginals in the tests and on this page are made up. Aggregates of very small areas can still disclose people: use published, disclosure-controlled tables, keep any microdata sample under the terms it was released with, and do not publish a synthetic population fitted to unpublished small counts.
