Decisions#

modelly.decide turns model runs into a comparison that a decision can rest on. It compares strategies with intervals, costs them against what they achieve, tests them across many possible futures, and searches a policy’s parameters. Every result comes from a seed, so the same study rerun gives the same tables.

from modelly.decide import Strategy, compare, cost_effectiveness, model_runner

run = model_runner(AgeSEIR, {"deaths": deaths}, scenario=SEIRScenario)
study = compare(
    run,
    [Strategy("nobody"), Strategy("seniors", {"vaccinated": (0, 0, 10_000)})],
    replicates=20,
    seed=7,
)
study.rank("deaths")  # best first, with intervals and how often each was best
study.difference("deaths")  # each strategy minus the first, paired by replicate

Comparing strategies#

A Strategy is a name, the parameters it sets and its cost: a number, or a function of one run’s outputs. compare(run, strategies, replicates=..., seed=...) runs each strategy replicates times. run(params, seed) builds and runs the model and returns its outputs; model_runner(model_class, outputs, scenario=...) writes that function for you.

Replicate r of every strategy gets the same seed: common random numbers. When the strategies consume the random numbers the same way, they face the same chance events (the same first infections, the same unlucky contacts), the difference between two strategies in one replicate is close to the effect of the strategy alone, and difference(output), the interval of those paired differences, is much narrower than the two separate intervals. When a strategy changes the order of the draws, the pairing buys little: measure it with modelly.explain.treatment_effect, which reports the variance reduction (see Causes and explanations). summary(output) gives each strategy’s mean and Student-t interval (95% by default, confidence= to change it). rank(output, lower_is_better=True) orders the strategies and adds best_share: the share of replicates in which each was the best, an estimate of the probability that it is the right choice.

The runs are independent, so workers= spreads them over processes: compare, robust and optimise take it (default 1, one run after another; None uses every core). The results come back in the order the loop gives, with the same seeds, so they are identical whatever workers is. run is sent to each worker; a closure such as model_runner’s needs Linux, where workers are forked. optimise runs one trial’s replicates in parallel and its trials in turn, since each trial depends on the ones before.

A worked example: who gets the vaccine?#

modelly.decide.vaccination is the study of this section. In the age-structured SEIR model (100,000 people in three age groups), 10,000 doses can be given before the epidemic to children, to adults, to seniors, or to nobody. The outputs are the infections and the deaths, each group’s infections times an illustrative fatality ratio (0.01%, 0.2%, 3%). The cost is 20 per dose plus 150 per infection for care.

from modelly.decide import cost_effectiveness, net_benefit
from modelly.decide.vaccination import vaccination_study

study = vaccination_study(replicates=20, seed=7)
study.rank("deaths")
cost_effectiveness(study, "deaths_averted")
net_benefit(study, "deaths_averted", wtp=100_000)

Vaccinating the children is the cheapest choice, cheaper than giving no doses at all, because children drive transmission and every infection they would have passed on costs care. Vaccinating the seniors averts the most deaths, at 9,942 per extra death averted over vaccinating the children. Seniors were the best choice in every replicate (best_share 1.0), and the intervals of the deaths averted are under four deaths wide. Here the pairing is not what makes them narrow: vaccinating adds a cohort row, which reorders the draws, so the arms are close to independent (variance ratio about 0.8); 100,000 people make each run’s result precise on its own. Run again with the same seed, the study gives the same tables (tests/decide/test_decide.py). The fatality ratios and costs are illustrative: the model’s card, at the end of its page, says what it must not be used for.

Cost-effectiveness#

cost_effectiveness(comparison, effect) places the strategies by mean cost and mean effect. A strategy is dominated when another costs no more and achieves at least as much, and extendedly dominated when a mix of two others would buy more effect for the same money. The remaining strategies form the efficient frontier. Each has an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (icer) over the previous one: the price of one more unit of effect. net_benefit(comparison, effect, wtp) sets a willingness to pay per unit of effect and ranks wtp * effect - cost per replicate, with intervals.

Robust decisions#

When the world’s parameters are uncertain, a strategy tuned to one best guess can fail elsewhere. robust runs every strategy in every future:

from modelly.decide import robust, sample_futures

futures = sample_futures({"beta": (0.025, 0.05), "recovery": (0.15, 0.3)}, 30, seed=1)
result = robust(run, strategies, futures, "deaths", replicates=5, threshold=300)
result.summary()  # mean, worst, max_regret, mean_regret, satisficing; minimax regret first

sample_futures draws futures by Latin hypercube sampling from ranges. Within a future every strategy gets the same seeds. The regret of a strategy in a future is how much worse it does than the best strategy there. summary() ranks by the largest regret (minimax regret), the usual criterion when the futures cannot be given probabilities. With a threshold it also reports the share of futures in which each strategy meets it.

Optimising an intervention#

optimise(run, space, objective, trials=50, seed=0) searches a policy’s parameters with Optuna’s TPE sampler (pip install "modelly-abm[decide]"). space maps names to ranges or lists of choices. Every proposal is evaluated on the same replicate seeds, and the sampler is seeded, so the same call gives the same trials and the same optimum:

from modelly.decide import optimise

best = optimise(
    run,
    {"vaccinated_seniors": (0, 20_000)},
    lambda out: out["deaths"],
    trials=40,
    seed=2,
)
best.params, best.value, best.trials

What these numbers are not#

A comparison is only as good as its model: intervals cover the model’s chance, not its assumptions. Read the model card before presenting a ranking, show the intervals with it, and say what was left out. The tables above never forecast what will happen; they say which strategy does best in the model, and by how much.
